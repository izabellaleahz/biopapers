I'm a scientist in the Gilbert lab at Arc Institute (functional genomics: CRISPR screens,
Perturb-seq, gene regulation). I trained with Caroline Uhler (causal ML, single-cell),
Nir Hacohen (tumor immunology, innate immunity) and Stefani Spranger (cancer immunology,
T cell priming), so I follow all three areas closely.

Core interests, roughly in priority order:
- Tumor immunology and T cell biology: priming, exhaustion, tissue-resident memory, antigen
  presentation, checkpoint and cell therapies, why tumors are immune-cold.
- CRISPR screens and Perturb-seq: new screening modalities, single-cell perturbation readouts,
  large perturbation atlases, and methods for analyzing them.
- Bio ML: virtual cell models, perturbation-response prediction, single-cell and genomic
  foundation models, causal inference from interventional data, protein design — especially
  work that is rigorously benchmarked rather than just bigger.
- B cells and antibodies, PhIP-seq and antibody-repertoire profiling, autoantibodies.
- Viral mimicry: endogenous retroelements, dsRNA sensing (MDA5, ADAR1, ZBP1), cGAS–STING,
  and how they shape tumor immunity.
- Trogocytosis and other membrane/antigen transfer between immune cells.
- Host–pathogen biology and virology, gene regulation and chromatin — when the finding is broad.

Causal inference is a core interest, from my training with Caroline Uhler: causal discovery and
structure learning (especially from interventional / perturbation data), causal representation
learning, identifiability, treatment-effect estimation and experimental design. I also follow graph
theory and network science: spectral and extremal graph theory, random graphs, graph algorithms,
and learning on graphs when it's principled rather than another GNN benchmark.

In ML / CS I want the papers that move the field: major new models, training methods, agent
and reasoning advances, interpretability findings, and evaluations that change how people think.
Incremental benchmark gains, narrow applications and routine workshop papers matter much less.

What makes a paper a must-read for me: a surprising mechanism, a new tool or dataset I could
use, a large perturbation or immune dataset, a method that changes how screens are designed or
analyzed, or a paper the whole field will be talking about. Reviews matter only when they're
from leaders in my core areas.
